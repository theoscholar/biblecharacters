Enoch — He Walked With God

“Enoch kept walking with the true God. Then he was no more, for God took him.”
Genesis 5:24

Focus. Only two men in the whole Bible are described as walking with God. Enoch is the first. He was not a soldier and he fought no battle, yet he is a study in courage — because he spent his entire adult life saying an unwelcome thing to people who did not want to hear it, and he did it alone.

1.Create context — when, who, and what was going on

When

Enoch was born in 3404 BCE and was taken by God in 3039 BCE, at the age of 365 (Gen. 5:23). That places him seven generations after Adam — Jude calls him “the seventh one in line from Adam” (Jude 14).

Two dates are worth holding on to. Adam did not die until 3096 BCE, which means Enoch shared the earth with the first man for over 300 years. And the Flood did not arrive until 2370 BCE — nearly 700 years after Enoch was gone. Enoch announced a judgment he would never see arrive.

Timeline — Enoch in the stream of events

What was going on

Genesis is brief about Enoch’s era, but Jude fills in the atmosphere. Enoch’s contemporaries are described four times over in a single sentence as ungodly — in their deeds, in the manner of those deeds, and in the “shocking things” they said (Jude 15). This was not quiet indifference to God. It was loud contempt, and Enoch was standing in the middle of it.

The world had not yet reached the state it would be in by Noah’s day, when it was “full of violence” (Gen. 6:13) — but it was travelling in that direction, and Enoch could see the road.

2.What was Jehovah’s view of the situation?

Heart of itJehovah was not a distant observer. He noticed one man in a hostile generation, kept company with him, and made sure he was rewarded.

Jehovah counted Enoch as a companion. The phrase “walked with the true God” is remarkable. It is not “obeyed God” or “believed in God.” Walking is what you do with a friend, over time, in the same direction. Genesis uses that expression of only two men — Enoch, and later Noah (Gen. 6:9).

Jehovah told him so. Enoch did not have to guess whether he was pleasing God. Paul says that “before he was transferred he received the witness that he had pleased God well” (Heb. 11:5). Somehow — by prophecy, by a vision, by the prophecy he was given to speak — Enoch was told plainly that God approved of him.

Hebrews 11:6

“Moreover, without faith it is impossible to please God well, for whoever approaches God must believe that he is and that he becomes the rewarder of those earnestly seeking him.”

Jehovah entrusted him with a message about the future. The prophecy Jude preserves is about a judgment that would come — “Jehovah came with his holy myriads to execute judgment against all” (Jude 14, 15). Notice that Enoch spoke of it as already accomplished. He was that certain.

Jehovah protected him at the end. Enoch lived a mere 365 years in an age when men lived to 900 and more. The plain reading is that his enemies were closing in and God spared him a violent death: “he was no more, for God took him” (Gen. 5:24). Hebrews adds that he was “transferred so as not to see death” (Heb. 11:5) — evidently God put him into a peaceful sleep before his opposers could reach him.

3.What was Enoch doing — and how did he cope?

Heart of itHis courage was not one brave moment. It was three centuries of small, consistent, unfashionable choices.

He kept walking. The Hebrew wording carries the sense of continuous action, and Genesis stresses it twice — he “continued to walk” for 300 years, and he “kept walking” (Gen. 5:22, 24). Nobody walks 300 years by accident. This is the courage of stamina rather than the courage of a single heroic act.

He said the hard thing out loud. Enoch’s message named his listeners’ conduct for what it was. That is a very different matter from privately disapproving. He had to look at people he knew and tell them that God would call them to account.

He raised a family at the same time. It is easy to picture Enoch as a lone prophet on a hillside. Genesis says he had sons and daughters (Gen. 5:22). He was doing all of this while being a husband and a father in a community that despised what he stood for.

How did he cope?

  • He was convinced God existed and noticed. Hebrews 11:6 is placed right beside Enoch’s account for a reason: he believed God “becomes the rewarder of those earnestly seeking him.” Not might reward. Becomes the rewarder.
  • He had the relationship before he had the assignment. The walking came first. When the hard message came, he was already close enough to God to carry it.
  • He measured his life by God’s opinion, not his neighbours’. By every social measure of his day, Enoch was a failure — nobody listened. By God’s measure he had “pleased God well.”

4.How can this account benefit me today?

Heart of itCourage is usually a direction you keep walking in, not a leap you make once.
  • You do not have to see the results. Enoch died 669 years before his prophecy came true. If you are speaking up about something and nothing seems to change, you are in good company. Faithfulness is the assignment; results belong to Jehovah.
  • Being the only one is survivable. There is no hint that anyone joined Enoch. Being the only one at school, at work, or in the family who holds a standard is hard, but it is not new, and it is not disqualifying.
  • Consistency beats intensity. Three hundred years of ordinary faithful days is what Genesis chose to record. Small daily choices — prayer, reading, kindness, honesty — are the walk.
  • God notices before anyone else does. Enoch was told he pleased God. You may not get a prophecy, but the principle stands: the One whose opinion decides your future is already watching, and he is pleased by faith.
  • Speaking plainly is a form of love. Enoch’s message sounds severe, but a warning given in time is a kindness. The same is true of the message we carry.

Ask yourself: Which is harder for me — doing the right thing once when it costs a lot, or doing it every day when nobody notices? Enoch’s record suggests the second is the real test.
