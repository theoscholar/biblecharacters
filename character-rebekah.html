Rebekah — She Ventured Into the Unknown

“They called Rebekah and said to her: ‘Will you go with this man?’ She replied: ‘I am willing to go.’”
Genesis 24:58

Focus. A young woman is asked whether she will leave her family for a country she has never seen, to marry a man she has never met. She is given no time to think. Her answer is four words long, and it changed the line that led to the Messiah.

1.Create context — when, who, and what was going on

When

Isaac was 40 when he married Rebekah (Gen. 25:20), which places this in 1878 BCE. Sarah had died three years earlier, and Genesis notes that “Isaac found comfort after the loss of his mother” (Gen. 24:67).

Rebekah lived in Paddan-aram, several hundred miles north-east of Canaan. Leaving meant she would in all likelihood never see her family again. There were no visits home. The account gives no hint that she ever did.

Timeline — a decision made in a day

What was going on

The servant had prayed for a sign, and it was a shrewd one. He asked that the right woman would not only give him a drink but offer to water his camels as well. Rebekah did exactly that: “I will also draw water for your camels until they are done drinking” (Gen. 24:19).

Consider what she had actually volunteered for. Ten thirsty camels after a long desert journey, watered by hand from a well, one jar at a time. Genesis captures the sheer labour of it: “she quickly emptied her jar into the drinking trough and ran again and again to the well” (Gen. 24:20). This was hours of heavy work for a stranger who had asked only for a sip.

The servant's reaction tells you how unusual this was. He “silently stared at her in amazement” (Gen. 24:21).

2.What was Jehovah’s view of the situation?

Heart of itJehovah answered the prayer before it was finished, and the sign he chose revealed character rather than appearance.

The answer came early. “Even before he finished speaking, Rebekah … came out with her water jar on her shoulder” (Gen. 24:15). Jehovah was not waiting to be persuaded.

The test was for character, not looks. Genesis does mention that she was beautiful (Gen. 24:16), but that is not what identified her. What identified her was hard work offered to a stranger with no expectation of return.

Genesis 24:19, 20

“When she finished giving him a drink, she said: ‘I will also draw water for your camels until they are done drinking.’ So she quickly emptied her jar into the drinking trough and ran again and again to the well to draw water, and she kept drawing water for all his camels.”

Her willingness was honoured for generations. The line from Rebekah runs through Jacob to the nation of Israel and ultimately to the Messiah. A single decision made at a well in one afternoon carried that far.

3.What was Rebekah doing — and how did she cope?

Heart of itShe worked hard when no one had asked her to, and she decided quickly when it counted.

She went beyond what was requested. The servant asked for “a little sip of water” (Gen. 24:17). She gave him that, and then volunteered several hours of labour. Nobody was watching who could reward her — which is precisely why it revealed who she was.

She answered for herself. Her family suggested a delay of about ten days. The question was put to her directly — “Will you go with this man?” — and she gave a four-word answer with no hedging (Gen. 24:58).

She left permanently. This was not a trip. She was leaving her parents, her brother, her language and her landscape, almost certainly for good.

How did she cope?

  • Hard work was already normal for her. The camels did not turn her into a diligent person; they revealed that she already was one.
  • She had evidence to act on. The servant had told the family the whole story (Gen. 24:66). Her decision was quick, but it was not uninformed.
  • She was willing before she was certain. “I am willing to go” is not the same as “I know how this ends.” She had no guarantees about Isaac at all.

4.How can this account benefit me today?

Heart of itCharacter shows up in the work nobody asked you to do — and opportunities often arrive with no time to deliberate.
  • Do the unasked work. Rebekah's future turned on effort she volunteered when there was no visible reason to. What you do when no one is assessing you is who you are.
  • Be someone who can decide. She did not need ten days. When a clear opportunity appears, chronic hesitation is its own answer.
  • Willingness matters more than certainty. She could not know how it would turn out. She could know that she was willing.
  • Small kindnesses are not small. An afternoon at a well placed her in the line of the promise. You rarely see the size of a kind act at the time you are doing it.
  • Leaving comfort is a real cost — count it, then go. She left family, home and country. The account never pretends that was easy.
  • Answer for yourself. Others offered to decide the timing for her. She spoke for herself, respectfully and clearly.

Ask yourself: What would someone learn about me from the jobs I do when there is no credit in them?
