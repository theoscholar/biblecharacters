At a glance. South Africa occupies the southern tip of the African continent, where the Atlantic and Indian Oceans meet. It is a land of dramatic contrasts — snow-capped mountains and subtropical coast, gleaming cities and vast game reserves, and a population of striking diversity that has earned it the name the "Rainbow Nation." It is also, for our purposes, one of the great fields of Kingdom activity in Africa, with well over a century of history behind the good news there.

1.History

The earliest inhabitants were the San hunter-gatherers and the Khoikhoi herders. From around the early centuries C.E., Bantu-speaking peoples — ancestors of today's Zulu, Xhosa, Sotho, and others — settled across much of the land. European settlement began in 1652, when the Dutch East India Company established a supply station at the Cape under Jan van Riebeeck; French Huguenots and others followed.

Britain took the Cape in the early 1800s, prompting many Dutch-descended Boers to move inland in the Great Trek of the 1830s. The discovery of diamonds at Kimberley (1867) and gold on the Witwatersrand (1886) transformed the region — and, as one historian put it, "overnight it turned a pastoral country into an industrial one." Rivalry over that wealth helped fuel the Anglo-Boer War. In 1910 the British colonies and Boer republics were joined into the Union of South Africa.

In 1948 the National Party won power and made racial separation official state policy. The word apartheid is Afrikaans for "separateness," and the system was thorough: a battery of racial laws classified every person by race and dictated where they could live, travel, work, own land, and attend school. The black majority was stripped of political rights, confined to designated townships and rural "homelands," and controlled by pass laws and daily humiliation, while the country's wealth remained overwhelmingly in white hands. The policy drew mounting international condemnation, economic sanctions, and decades of internal unrest.

Apartheid also had religious underpinnings. The Dutch Reformed Church — the church of most Afrikaners — long lent it theological support. As Nelson Mandela himself observed, the policy "was supported by the Dutch Reformed Church, which furnished apartheid with its religious underpinnings by suggesting that Afrikaners were God's chosen people and that blacks were a subservient species. In the Afrikaner's world view, apartheid and the church went hand in hand." Only in October 1986 did the church's synod open its membership to all races and admit it had been wrong to use the Bible to justify apartheid; and in 1990, at a national church conference in Rustenburg, the Dutch Reformed theologian Professor Willie Jonker publicly confessed the church's guilt for the injustices committed — the church later apologizing openly for its role.

After decades of struggle, apartheid was dismantled in the early 1990s. In 1994 South Africa held its first fully democratic election, bringing Nelson Mandela to the presidency and giving birth to the "Rainbow Nation." Today the country is a constitutional democracy; Cyril Ramaphosa serves as president, leading a coalition government formed after the 2024 election. Against this backdrop of enforced division, Jehovah's Witnesses had already charted a strikingly different course — one of genuine racial unity in worship (see section 6).

2.Geography

South Africa is large — about four times the area of the British Isles — and geographically varied. A narrow coastal lowland rings the country, backed by mountains that rise to a vast interior plateau (the Highveld) making up most of the land. The plateau reaches its greatest height in the east, where the Drakensberg range climbs above 11,000 feet. The country wraps around two independent neighbors, Lesotho and Eswatini, and borders Namibia, Botswana, Zimbabwe, and Mozambique.

The far southwest, around Cape Town and iconic Table Mountain, enjoys a Mediterranean climate — wet winters, dry summers, and the famous Cape winelands. The interior plateau is different: summer thunderstorms bring refreshing coolness, while winters are dry, sunny, and mild by day. The northeast holds the celebrated Kruger National Park, one of Africa's greatest game reserves, home to the "Big Five." Two oceans meet near the Cape, where the cold Atlantic and warm Indian currents produce rich marine life.

3.People

South Africa's roughly 63–65 million people make it one of the most diverse nations on earth. By the government's broad categories, the population is about four-fifths black African (Zulu, Xhosa, Sotho, Pedi, Tswana, Swazi, Venda, Tsonga, Ndebele, and others), together with sizable white (English- and Afrikaans-speaking), colored (mixed-heritage), and Indian/Asian communities. This "kaleidoscope of skin color" and profusion of tongues is exactly what gives the country its "Rainbow Nation" character.

4.Language

South Africa recognizes twelve official languages — the historic eleven plus South African Sign Language, added in 2023. Listed roughly by number of speakers, they are Zulu, Xhosa, Afrikaans, Sepedi (Northern Sotho), English, Setswana, Sesotho, Xitsonga, siSwati, Tshivenda, and isiNdebele. English, though the first language of only a small minority, serves as the common language of government, business, and travel, so visitors get by easily. A friendly "Sawubona" (hello, in Zulu) or "Molo" (Xhosa) is always appreciated.

5.Culture

For all its past divisions, South African culture is marked by the ethic of ubuntu — "I am because we are" — a deep sense of shared humanity and community. Daily life blends African, European, and Asian influences: the braai (barbecue) is a national institution; the music runs from Zulu choral and gospel to jazz and kwaito; and cuisine ranges from Cape Malay curries to biltong and bobotie. Sport — especially rugby, cricket, and soccer — is a powerful unifier, as the nation's shared celebrations have repeatedly shown. Visitors typically find South Africans warm, welcoming, and proud of their country's hard-won diversity.

6.How the Good News Reached South Africa

The first seeds of Bible truth arrived in 1902, carried — remarkably — in the luggage of a clergyman returning from Holland. One of his boxes contained publications of the Bible Students, as Jehovah's Witnesses were then known. These came into the hands of Frans Ebersohn and Stoffel Fourie in Klerksdorp, who recognized the truth and at once began sharing it with others. The effect echoed for generations: more than 80 relatives of the Fourie family across five generations, along with many Ebersohns, became dedicated servants of Jehovah.

In 1910, William W. Johnston of Glasgow, Scotland — sober-minded and reliable, then in his early 30s — arrived with instructions to open a branch office, which he set up in a small room. When he was reassigned to Australia in 1918, Henry Anckerill, a retired former member of Natal's legislative assembly, took over the branch for the next six years. Two brothers then came from Britain to help: Thomas Walder, who became branch overseer, and George Phillips, who succeeded him and served for nearly 40 years, making a major contribution to establishing the Kingdom work across the country.

Milestones followed. In January 1939, Consolation (now Awake!) was printed in Afrikaans — the first magazine ever produced by Jehovah's Witnesses in South Africa, its type set by hand. The 1940 service year saw a new peak of 881 publishers, a 58.7 percent jump over the year before. In 1968 the book The Truth That Leads to Eternal Life — affectionately called "the blue bombshell" — sparked explosive growth; the branch's annual book shipments leapt from about 90,000 to 447,000 within two years.

Perhaps most striking, in the depths of the apartheid era Jehovah's Witnesses set aside the color bar in their own worship. The high point came on Sunday, January 6, 1974, at the "Divine Victory" International Assembly. Because segregation laws forbade mixed gatherings, delegates first met for four days in separate venues by race and language — but permission was granted for everyone to assemble together for the final session at Rand Stadium in Johannesburg, the only venue in the city where interracial gatherings were allowed. An audience of 33,408 of every race worshipped as one, the program delivered in English and translated into Afrikaans, Portuguese, Sesotho, and Zulu. Brother Keith Wiggill, then 23, recalled: "It was an unbelievable occasion and an answer to many prayers that we could all worship together… I felt as one with my brothers and sisters." The organization looks back on that day as a "divine victory" over racial segregation — a living demonstration that, in the Christian congregation, God "is not partial."—Acts 10:34, 35.

A descendant of the Fourie family serves at the South Africa Bethel to this day.

8.The Current State of Our Brotherhood

South Africa is today one of the largest fields of Kingdom activity in Africa: 107,746 publishers meet in 1,925 congregations, a ratio of roughly one Witness to every 600 people — a striking increase from the 881 publishers of 1940. The branch office is located at Krugersdorp, near Johannesburg.

The work is intensely multilingual, matching the nation's diversity. Kingdom proclamation in Zulu has now passed the 75-year mark, and the branch translates into many of the country's languages, with dedicated translation work for Tshivenda and South African Sign Language, among others. Special campaigns continue to carry the good news to Sesotho-, Zulu-, and Xhosa-speaking people in isolated areas. In 2019 Johannesburg hosted the "Love Never Fails"! international convention. From two men in Klerksdorp to a brotherhood of over a hundred thousand, the growth in South Africa is a powerful testimony that Jehovah "makes it grow."
