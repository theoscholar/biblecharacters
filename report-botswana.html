At a glance. Botswana is a landlocked, sparsely populated country in southern Africa, famous for stable democracy, diamonds, and some of the finest wildlife country on the continent. Your destination, Kasane, sits in the far north at the meeting point of four nations, at the gateway to Chobe National Park and just a short hop from Victoria Falls. It is also a place with a special history for Jehovah's people, as you will see below.

1.History

The earliest inhabitants of the region were the San (also called Basarwa or "Bushmen"), hunter-gatherers whose presence in the Kalahari goes back many thousands of years. From roughly the 17th century onward, Tswana-speaking peoples migrated into the area and organized themselves into several chiefdoms (the Bangwato, Bakwena, Bangwaketse, and others) centered on large cattle-keeping villages.

In 1885, at the request of Tswana chiefs seeking protection from Boer expansion and German ambitions in the region, Britain declared the Bechuanaland Protectorate. Notably, three chiefs — Khama III, Sebele, and Bathoen — travelled to England in 1895 to petition against having their land handed to Cecil Rhodes's British South Africa Company, and largely succeeded in preserving local autonomy.

Independence came peacefully on September 30, 1966, when the protectorate became the Republic of Botswana. Its first president, Sir Seretse Khama — heir to the Bangwato chieftainship, whose interracial marriage had earlier caused an imperial controversy — set the country on a course of multiparty democracy and prudent governance. The discovery of diamonds at Orapa in 1967 (and later Jwaneng) transformed one of the world's poorest nations into a stable, upper-middle-income country. Botswana has since held regular elections; the 2024 election brought Duma Boko to the presidency, ending nearly six decades of rule by the founding party.

2.Geography

Botswana is landlocked, bordered by South Africa to the south and southeast, Namibia to the west and north, Zimbabwe to the northeast, and meeting Zambia at a single point on the Zambezi near Kasane. The Kalahari Desert — actually a vast semi-arid sand savanna rather than a barren desert — blankets roughly 70 percent of the land. Much of the country is flat, and water is precious; the very name of the currency, pula, means "rain."

The north, where you are headed, holds the country's natural treasures. The Okavango Delta is one of the world's largest inland deltas — a river that empties not into a sea but into the Kalahari sands, creating a shifting wetland teeming with wildlife (a UNESCO World Heritage Site). To the northeast lies Chobe National Park, renowned for enormous elephant herds. Nearby are the Makgadikgadi salt pans, remnants of an ancient super-lake.

A 1991 traveler's account in Awake! captured the Delta's magic: "a unique watery world that begins as suddenly as it ends, on the vast sands of the Kalahari Desert in northern Botswana." Though only about the size of Northern Ireland, it is "lost on the 100,000 square miles of trackless, undisturbed Kalahari" — fed by the Okavango River, which rises in the highlands of central Angola and turns "a parched desert into a verdant garden." The traditional way to travel its reed-lined channels is the mokoro, a dugout canoe hewn from a local tree — well worth experiencing if your itinerary allows.

Kasane and Kazungula. Your base, Kasane, lies on the Chobe River at the extraordinary Kazungula quadripoint, where Botswana, Namibia, Zambia, and Zimbabwe converge. It is the launching point for Chobe game drives and river cruises, and Victoria Falls — "Mosi-oa-Tunya," the smoke that thunders, on the Zambezi at the Zambia–Zimbabwe border — is only about 80 km (an hour or so) away. Expect elephants, hippos, buffalo, and abundant birdlife right along the river.

3.People and Language

Botswana is one of the most sparsely populated countries in Africa — about 2.6 million people in a land the size of France or Texas. A citizen is a Motswana (plural Batswana); the same word also names the dominant ethnic group. Alongside the Tswana majority live the Kalanga, the San, the Bakgalagadi, Herero, and others.

The national language is Setswana, spoken by the great majority, while English is the official language of government, business, and secondary education — so you will find English widely understood, especially in towns and tourist areas like Kasane. Common courtesies go a long way: Dumela rra (hello, sir) and Dumela mma (hello, madam), and Pula! as an all-purpose exclamation of goodwill.

4.Culture

Batswana culture prizes consensus, courtesy, and community. A central traditional institution is the kgotla, a public assembly where villagers gather with their chief to discuss matters and settle disputes by open consensus — an early form of grass-roots democracy that still shapes national life. The guiding ethic of botho (akin to ubuntu: "I am because we are") emphasizes dignity, respect, and mutual care.

Cattle hold deep cultural and economic importance, historically the measure of a family's standing and still woven into custom and celebration. Botswana is also known for its calm, orderly, and famously peaceful society, its choral and traditional music, and its handcrafts — the coiled baskets of the northwest are especially prized. Visitors typically find Batswana reserved but warm, and unfailingly polite.

5.How the Good News Reached Botswana

Kingdom truth first entered the territory — then known as Bechuanaland — in 1929, when, as the 2007 Yearbook notes, a lone brother "shared in the preaching work there for a few months." Growth came against real opposition. In 1941, wartime political hysteria led the colonial government to restrict the preaching work and ban the distribution of Bible literature. Those restrictions were finally lifted in 1959.

Into that difficult period stepped a South African circuit overseer, Joshua Thongoana, who was assigned to the Bechuanaland circuit in 1956. He recalled that "the Society's publications were under ban in Botswana, so our house-to-house preaching was done cautiously, without using the Society's literature." On one occasion he and local Witnesses were arrested near the village of Maphashalala; a chief ordered the local brothers flogged, but — remarkably — a clergyman pleaded for leniency and they were released. When Thongoana arrived there were about 154 publishers; by the time the ban lifted three years later, the figure had grown to 192.

Freedom was not permanent. In 1972 a new law required all organizations to register; when the brothers applied, their application was denied and they were given just 20 days to cease all activity. Jehovah's Witnesses were again banned, and participation could bring up to seven years' imprisonment. The brothers kept meeting in secret — sometimes in secluded spots out in the bush. As Brother Tommy Maruping recalled, "Despite the ban, we were more determined than ever to continue preaching and meeting together. We trusted that Jehovah would remain with us, and he always did."

Then, on February 20, 1974, the government reversed course and Jehovah's Witnesses were granted legal registration — a freedom celebrated at its 50th anniversary in 2024.

6.Notable Individuals Who Spearheaded the Work

Joshua ThongoanaCircuit overseer, from 1956

Born in 1912 near Pietersburg, South Africa, he became a traveling overseer and in 1956 took up the demanding circuit work across Bechuanaland — traveling by train or on the floor of open trucks, preaching cautiously under a literature ban, and enduring arrest. He later served as an instructor in the Kingdom Ministry School and the Pioneer Service School. His life story, "I Found Satisfaction in Serving God," appeared in The Watchtower of February 1, 1993 (pages 25-9). The 2007 Yearbook pictures "the Thongoanas" still witnessing to a street vendor decades later.

Andrew and Georgina MasondoCircuit work, from 1965

Andrew was appointed a circuit overseer in 1954 and assigned to Botswana in 1965 — an assignment he said "was just like a missionary assignment." A three-year drought had brought famine, and he and Georgina, he recalled, "found out what it is like to go to bed without supper" and still go out in the field ministry the next day.

Schalk CoetzeeReached the Kasane group, mid-1980s

He led a small party of pioneers north through the Chobe Game Reserve — past buffalo and elephant herds, with lions roaring at their night camps — to visit the isolated group of Witnesses at Kasane. Finding them meeting in a mud hut, he encouraged them to build their own Kingdom Hall. (See section 7.)

Tommy MarupingEndured the 1972–74 ban

One of the local brothers who kept the congregation alive during the ban, meeting in secret in the bush. His determination — "we were more determined than ever" — typifies the faith that carried the brotherhood through to legal recognition in 1974.

Hugh and Carol CormicanMissionaries, from just after 1974

Hugh began missionary service shortly after the ban was lifted, serving with Carol in the south of the country. He loved to tell of young ones like Eddie, a 12-year-old who learned to read so he could join the ministry and, after baptism, often auxiliary pioneered. Decades on, Hugh could testify that "our brothers and sisters have continued to thrive spiritually until this day," enjoying the unity that comes from meeting freely.

Botswana has been blessed by a succession of dedicated missionaries who learned Setswana to reach the people: Blake and Gwen Frisbee, Tim and Virginia Crouch, and — serving in the north — Veijo and Sirpa Kuismin, among others named in the 2007 Yearbook.

7.Kasane and the North — For Your Visit

Kasane figures directly in our modern history. A 1986 account describes a party of pioneers who, after serving in Namibia's Caprivi Strip, pressed on to "the isolated group at Kasane in northern Botswana," traveling "through the Chobe Game Reserve, at times alongside large buffalo and elephant herds," and camping where "they heard lions roaring nearby."

There they preached "from hut to hut, African style" — a slow, dignified approach in which the visitor stands outside and calls out, is invited in, and only after warm greetings and inquiries about health and family begins to share a Bible thought. The local Witnesses, then meeting in a mud hut, were "very happy and grateful that the party had come so far to visit them." As you take in Chobe's elephants and the Zambezi's quiet — "broken only by the throb of distant drums, the heartbeat of Africa" — you will be standing where zealous brothers and sisters carried the good news under conditions few of us can imagine.

8.The Current State of Our Brotherhood

Today Botswana is home to 2,473 publishers meeting in 43 congregations — a ratio of roughly one Witness to every 1,002 people, a strong proportion for the region and the fruit of decades of patient effort. Growth has been steady: from about 777 Witnesses in 1993, to well over 1,700 by 2009, to today's total.

Most congregations cluster in and around the capital, Gaborone, in the densely populated east. Reaching the rest of the country means long journeys west into scattered villages and the Kalahari — where a few San families still roam, living off the land and hunting with bow and arrow. Publishers have exerted great effort in special campaigns, traveling thousands of miles to bring Bible truths to remote cattle posts and nomadic families.

The field is also increasingly multilingual. Meetings are held in Botswana Sign Language, English, Kalanga, Setswana, and Shona, reflecting the mix of peoples the good news now reaches. A regional convention with the theme "Exercise Patience"! was held in the capital, Gaborone, in 2023, and in February 2024 the brothers joyfully marked fifty years of religious freedom. The endurance of Jehovah's people there — through wartime restriction, outright ban, and secret meetings in the bush — is a living illustration that "his commandments are not burdensome."—1 John 5:3.
