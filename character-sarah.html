“And yet, if they had kept remembering the place from which they had departed, they would have had opportunity to return.”
Hebrews 11:15

Focus. Sarah left a settled city for a tent and never went back — and the striking thing is that she could have. The road to Ur stayed open for over 60 years. Every year she did not take it was a decision.

1.Create context — when, who, and what was going on

When

Sarah was about ten years younger than Abraham (Gen. 17:17), so she was born around 2008 BCE. She left Haran in 1943 BCE aged about 65, and died at 127 in 1881 BCE (Gen. 23:1).

Hold on to the arithmetic, because it is the whole point: Sarah lived as a foreigner in tents for 62 years. Isaac was not born until she was 90 — roughly 25 years after leaving home before she saw the first instalment of what she had been promised.

What was going on

Ur was not a village. It was a wealthy, literate, well-supplied city, and Sarah was leaving it for a portable life among strangers. Genesis flags her deepest sorrow before it tells us anything else about her: “Now Sarai was barren; she had no child” (Gen. 11:30).

So the promise she was asked to believe — that a great nation would come from this family (Gen. 12:2) — struck directly at the one thing that had already failed. She was asked to leave security for a promise built on her weakest point.

2.What was Jehovah’s view of the situation?
Heart of itJehovah did not merely tolerate Sarah's presence on the journey. He renamed her, credited her with faith, and recorded her laughter without condemning her for it.

He gave her a new name. “Sarah will become her name” (Gen. 17:15) — princess. In the same breath: “she will become nations; kings of peoples will come from her” (Gen. 17:16). The promise was not made about her; it was made to her.

He answered her laugh with a question, not a rebuke. “Is anything too extraordinary for Jehovah?” (Gen. 18:14) That is a correction aimed at enlarging her view of God rather than shaming her.

Hebrews 11:11

“By faith also Sarah received power to conceive offspring, even when she was past the age, since she considered Him faithful who made the promise.”

He counted her among the faithful. Peter holds her up as a pattern for “the holy women of the past who hoped in God” (1 Pet. 3:5), and Hebrews says of that group that “God is not ashamed of them, to be called on as their God” (Heb. 11:16).

3.What was Sarah doing — and how did she cope?
Heart of itShe kept not going back. That sounds passive, but over 62 years it is one of the most demanding things a person can do.

She went in the first place. She had to agree to a move with no destination named — “to the land that I will show you” (Gen. 12:1). She packed for a journey whose endpoint nobody could tell her.

She stayed gone. The door home was never locked (Heb. 11:15). Sarah simply stopped looking at it.

She got some things wrong. The Hagar arrangement was Sarah's idea, and it produced years of friction. Scripture does not tidy this up — which is itself encouraging.

How did she cope?

  • She judged the promise by the One who made it. She “considered Him faithful.” Abraham is described the same way — convinced God was able to do what he had said.
    Romans 4:20, 21 — “he did not waver in a lack of faith; but he became powerful by his faith, giving God glory and being fully convinced that what He had promised He was also able to do.”
  • She did not look behind her. Jesus later used exactly this picture for someone unfit for the work ahead.
    Luke 9:62 — “No man who has put his hand to a plow and looks at the things behind is well-suited for the Kingdom of God.”
  • She endured a very long wait. Twenty-five years between promise and fulfilment is a lifetime of practising three things at once.
    Romans 12:12 — “Rejoice in the hope. Endure under tribulation. Persevere in prayer.”
  • She kept a sense of humour. When Isaac arrived she said God had brought her laughter (Gen. 21:6). The same laughter, transformed.
4.How can this account benefit me today?
Heart of itThe test is rarely the moment you leave. It is the sixty years afterwards when going back would still be easy.
  • Leaving is a decision you make repeatedly. Whatever you walked away from, the road back stays open. Not taking it, again and again, is the actual faithfulness.
    Luke 9:62 — “No man who has put his hand to a plow and looks at the things behind is well-suited for the Kingdom of God.”
  • Homesickness is not failure. Hebrews does not say they never thought of home — only that they did not keep remembering it in a way that pulled them back.
    Hebrews 11:16 — “But now they are reaching out for a better place, that is, one belonging to heaven. Therefore, God is not ashamed of them, to be called on as their God, for he has prepared a city for them.”
  • God's promises often land on your weakest spot. Sarah was barren and was promised nations. That is where God's power is most visible.
    2 Corinthians 12:9 — “My undeserved kindness is sufficient for you, for my power is being made perfect in weakness.”
  • Waiting is not wasted time. Twenty-five years passed before Isaac. Nothing suggests those years were a detour.
    James 1:3, 4 — “knowing as you do that this tested quality of your faith produces endurance. But let endurance complete its work, so that you may be complete and sound in all respects, not lacking in anything.”
  • You can doubt and still be listed among the faithful. Sarah laughed. She is still in Hebrews 11. A moment of disbelief does not cancel a lifetime of trust.

Ask yourself: What is the “Ur” I keep glancing back at — and what would it cost me to stop looking?
