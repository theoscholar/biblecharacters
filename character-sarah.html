Sarah — She Refused to Look Back

“And yet, if they had kept remembering the place from which they had departed, they would have had opportunity to return.”
Hebrews 11:15

Focus. Sarah left a settled city for a tent and never went back — and the striking thing is that she could have. The road to Ur stayed open for over 60 years. Every year she did not take it was a decision.

1.Create context — when, who, and what was going on

When

Sarah was about ten years younger than Abraham (Gen. 17:17), which places her birth around 2008 BCE. She left Haran with Abraham in 1943 BCE, when he was 75 (Gen. 12:4) and she was about 65. She died at 127 in 1881 BCE (Gen. 23:1).

Hold on to the arithmetic, because it is the whole point of the chapter: Sarah lived as a foreigner in tents for 62 years. Isaac was not born until she was 90  (Gen. 17:17) — which means she waited roughly 25 years after leaving home before she saw the first small instalment of what she had been promised.

What was going on

Ur was not a village. It was a wealthy, literate, well-supplied city, and Sarah was leaving it for a portable life among strangers. Genesis flags her deepest sorrow before it tells us anything else about her: “Now Sarai was barren; she had no child” (Gen. 11:30). In her culture, that was not merely a private grief; it carried public shame.

So the promise she was asked to believe — that a great nation would come from this family (Gen. 12:2) — struck directly at the one thing that had already failed. She was asked to leave security for a promise built on her weakest point.

2.What was Jehovah’s view of the situation?

Heart of itJehovah did not merely tolerate Sarah's presence on the journey. He renamed her, credited her with faith, and recorded her laughter without condemning her for it.

He gave her a new name. “You must not call her Sarai, because Sarah will become her name” (Gen. 17:15). The name means princess. In the same breath he said, “she will become nations; kings of peoples will come from her” (Gen. 17:16). The promise was not made about her; it was made to her.

He answered her laugh with a question, not a rebuke. When Sarah laughed to herself at the idea (Gen. 18:12), Jehovah's reply was “Is anything too extraordinary for Jehovah?” (Gen. 18:14) That is a correction, but a gentle one — aimed at enlarging her view of God rather than shaming her.

Hebrews 11:11

“By faith also Sarah received power to conceive offspring, even when she was past the age, since she considered Him faithful who made the promise.”

He counted her among the faithful. Centuries later Paul lists her by name, and Peter holds her up as a pattern for “the holy women of the past who hoped in God” (1 Pet. 3:5). Hebrews says of that whole group that “God is not ashamed of them, to be called on as their God” (Heb. 11:16).

3.What was Sarah doing — and how did she cope?

Heart of itShe kept not going back. That sounds passive, but over 62 years it is one of the most demanding things a person can do.

She went in the first place. Genesis records Abraham's call, but Sarah had to agree to a move with no destination named — “to the land that I will show you” (Gen. 12:1). She packed for a journey whose endpoint nobody could tell her.

She stayed gone. This is the argument Hebrews makes. If they had “kept remembering the place from which they had departed, they would have had opportunity to return” (Heb. 11:15). The door home was never locked. Sarah simply stopped looking at it.

She got some things wrong. The Hagar arrangement was Sarah's idea, and it produced years of friction. Scripture does not tidy this up. Her faith is commended without her record being made spotless — which is itself encouraging.

How did she cope?

  • She judged the promise by the One who made it. Hebrews puts it precisely: she “considered Him faithful who made the promise” (Heb. 11:11). She did not assess the odds; she assessed God.
  • She let herself be corrected. The laugh was real, and so was the change of mind afterwards.
  • She kept a sense of humour. When Isaac finally arrived she said, “God has brought me laughter; everybody hearing of it will laugh with me” (Gen. 21:6). The same laughter, transformed.
  • She was not alone. She and Abraham carried this together for decades.

4.How can this account benefit me today?

Heart of itThe test is rarely the moment you leave. It is the sixty years afterwards when going back would still be easy.
  • Leaving is a decision you make repeatedly. Whatever you have walked away from — a career path, a crowd, a habit — the road back stays open. Not taking it, again and again, is the actual faithfulness.
  • Homesickness is not failure. Hebrews does not say they never thought of home. It says they did not keep remembering it in a way that pulled them back. Feeling the pull and not following it is exactly the point.
  • God's promises often land on your weakest spot. Sarah was barren and was promised nations. That is not cruelty; it is the place where God's power is most visible.
  • Waiting is not wasted time. Twenty-five years passed between the promise and Isaac. Nothing in the record suggests those years were a detour.
  • You can doubt and still be listed among the faithful. Sarah laughed. She is still in Hebrews 11. A moment of disbelief does not cancel a lifetime of trust.

Ask yourself: What is the “Ur” I keep glancing back at — and what would it cost me to stop looking?
