Abraham — The First Man to Wage a Righteous War

Bible reading study note · Genesis 14:11-24; Hebrews 7:1, 2

“Thus Abram heard that his relative had been taken captive. With that he mobilized his trained men, 318 servants born in his household, and went in pursuit up to Dan.”
Genesis 14:14

Focus. A peaceable herdsman with no army chases down a victorious coalition to rescue one nephew — then refuses every reward on offer. The courage in this account is impressive; the self-control afterwards is arguably greater.

1.Create context — when, who, and what was going on

When

This happened after Lot had settled in Sodom and while Abraham was still called Abram — so before the covenant sign of 1919 BCE. It is usually placed around 1933 BCE, roughly a decade after the family entered Canaan.

The setting is a regional war. Four allied kings under Chedorlaomer had held the cities of the Jordan plain as vassals, put down a rebellion, sacked Sodom and Gomorrah, and left with the plunder (Gen. 14:11). Lot went with it (Gen. 14:12).

Timeline — the rescue and its aftermath

1943 BCE Abram enters Canaan at 75 (Gen. 12:4).
c. 1938 BCE Abram and Lot separate; Lot chooses the Jordan plain and moves toward Sodom.
c. 1933 BCE Four kings sack Sodom and carry Lot off (Gen. 14:11, 12).
c. 1933 BCE Abram pursues with 318 men, attacks by night, and recovers everyone (Gen. 14:14-16).
c. 1933 BCE Melchizedek blesses him; Abram gives a tenth and refuses Sodom's goods (Gen. 14:18-23).
1919 BCE The covenant is formalised; Abram becomes Abraham.

What was going on

Abraham was not a soldier and had no quarrel with these kings. He was a wealthy herdsman whose life was flocks, tents and hospitality. What reached him was simply news: “a man who had escaped came and told Abram the Hebrew” (Gen. 14:13).

The military odds were poor. A coalition that had just defeated several kingdoms was returning north with its plunder. Against that, Abraham had 318 trained men from his own household plus three allied brothers. He also had one advantage: nobody expected him.

2.What was Jehovah’s view of the situation?

Heart of itJehovah backed this action — but notice what kind of action it was. A rescue with a defined objective, not a campaign of conquest.

God gave the victory, and Melchizedek said so. The blessing credits the win to God rather than to tactics: “praised be the Most High God, Who has handed your oppressors over to you!” (Gen. 14:20).

It carried unusual weight. Melchizedek was “king of Salem, priest of the Most High God” (Heb. 7:1), and Hebrews later uses him as a picture of Christ's kingship and priesthood. That such a figure came out to meet Abraham says a great deal about how this rescue was regarded.

Genesis 14:19, 20

“Then he blessed him and said: ‘Blessed be Abram by the Most High God, Maker of heaven and earth; And praised be the Most High God, Who has handed your oppressors over to you!’ And Abram gave him a tenth of everything.”

The war had a limit. Abraham did not press on to take territory or set himself up as a regional power. He recovered “all the goods, and he also recovered Lot his relative, his goods, the women, and the other people” (Gen. 14:16) — and stopped. The objective defined the action.

3.What was Abraham doing — and how did he cope?

Heart of itHe moved immediately, fought intelligently, and then turned down the payday.

He acted at once. There is no interval in the account between hearing and mobilising. Lot had chosen Sodom over Abraham's company years earlier, and had arguably brought this on himself. Abraham does not appear to have weighed that for a moment.

He fought with his head. “During the night, he divided his forces, and he and his servants attacked and defeated them” (Gen. 14:15). A night attack from multiple directions against a larger, complacent force carrying plunder. Courage here is not recklessness — it is planning.

He refused the reward. The king of Sodom's offer was generous and entirely customary: keep the goods, return the people (Gen. 14:21). Abraham had already sworn an oath, and his reasoning is worth reading twice — he would take nothing “from a thread to a sandal lace, so that you may not say, ‘I made Abram rich’” (Gen. 14:23). He would not let anyone else claim credit for blessings that came from Jehovah.

How did he cope?

  • He had decided in advance. The oath was raised before the offer was made (Gen. 14:22). He was not improvising under pressure.
  • He acknowledged the source of the victory by giving Melchizedek a tenth before anyone discussed his share.
  • He did not impose his conscience on others. His allies were free to take their portion: “let them take their share” (Gen. 14:24). A personal standard, held personally.

4.How can this account benefit me today?

Heart of itReal courage usually has a boundary around it. Abraham knew what he was fighting for, and he knew when to stop.
  • Be quick when someone needs help. Whether Lot deserved rescuing never enters the account. Family in trouble was enough.
  • Courage and planning are not opposites. Dividing his forces and attacking at night was smart, not timid. Wisdom does not dilute bravery.
  • Decide your limits before you are offered anything. Abraham's oath was already sworn. Most compromises happen because the decision was left until the moment of pressure.
  • Guard who gets the credit. He refused wealth rather than let a man of Sodom claim to have enriched him. What people conclude about the source of your blessings is worth protecting.
  • Hold your standards without policing others. He did not require Aner, Eshcol and Mamre to refuse their share.
  • Know when the objective is met. He rescued and went home. He did not turn a justified action into an appetite.

Ask yourself: Have I actually decided, in advance, what I will not accept — or am I planning to work it out when the offer arrives?
