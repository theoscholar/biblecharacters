Abraham — The First Man to Wage a Righteous War

“Thus Abram heard that his relative had been taken captive. With that he mobilized his trained men, 318 servants born in his household, and went in pursuit up to Dan.”
Genesis 14:14

Focus. A peaceable herdsman with no army chases down a victorious coalition to rescue one nephew — then refuses every reward on offer. The courage is impressive; the self-control afterwards is arguably greater.

1.Create context — when, who, and what was going on

When

This happened while Abraham was still called Abram — so before the covenant sign of 1919 BCE. It is usually placed around 1933 BCE, roughly a decade after the family entered Canaan.

The setting is a regional war. Four allied kings under Chedorlaomer had put down a rebellion, sacked Sodom and Gomorrah, and left with the plunder (Gen. 14:11). Lot went with it (Gen. 14:12).

Timeline — the rescue and its aftermath

1943 BCE Abram enters Canaan at 75 (Gen. 12:4).
c. 1938 BCE Abram and Lot separate; Lot moves toward Sodom.
c. 1933 BCE Four kings sack Sodom and carry Lot off (Gen. 14:11, 12).
c. 1933 BCE Abram pursues with 318 men, attacks by night, and recovers everyone (Gen. 14:14-16).
c. 1933 BCE Melchizedek blesses him; Abram gives a tenth and refuses Sodom's goods (Gen. 14:18-23).
1919 BCE The covenant is formalised; Abram becomes Abraham.

What was going on

Abraham was not a soldier and had no quarrel with these kings. What reached him was simply news: “a man who had escaped came and told Abram the Hebrew” (Gen. 14:13).

The military odds were poor. A coalition that had just defeated several kingdoms was returning north with its plunder. Against that, Abraham had 318 trained men plus three allied brothers. He also had one advantage: nobody expected him.

2.What was Jehovah’s view of the situation?
Heart of itJehovah backed this action — but notice what kind of action it was. A rescue with a defined objective, not a campaign of conquest.

God gave the victory, and Melchizedek said so. The blessing credits the win to God rather than to tactics (Gen. 14:20).

It carried unusual weight. Melchizedek was “king of Salem, priest of the Most High God” (Heb. 7:1), and Hebrews later uses him as a picture of Christ's kingship and priesthood.

Genesis 14:19, 20

“Then he blessed him and said: ‘Blessed be Abram by the Most High God, Maker of heaven and earth; And praised be the Most High God, Who has handed your oppressors over to you!’ And Abram gave him a tenth of everything.”

The war had a limit. Abraham did not press on to take territory. He recovered the people and goods (Gen. 14:16) — and stopped. The objective defined the action.

3.What was Abraham doing — and how did he cope?
Heart of itHe moved immediately, fought intelligently, and then turned down the payday.

He acted at once. There is no interval between hearing and mobilising. Lot had chosen Sodom over Abraham's company years earlier, and had arguably brought this on himself. Abraham does not appear to have weighed that for a moment.

He fought with his head. “During the night, he divided his forces” (Gen. 14:15). Courage here is not recklessness — it is planning.

He refused the reward. He would take nothing “from a thread to a sandal lace, so that you may not say, ‘I made Abram rich’” (Gen. 14:23). He would not let anyone else claim credit for blessings that came from Jehovah.

How did he cope?

  • He had decided in advance. The oath was raised before the offer was made (Gen. 14:22). He was not improvising under pressure.
    Proverbs 22:1 — “A good name is to be chosen rather than great wealth; To be respected is better than silver and gold.”
  • He trusted the right source of strength. Numbers were against him, and the credit went where it belonged.
    Psalms 20:7 — “Some rely on chariots and others on horses, But we call on the name of Jehovah our God.”
  • He understood what money does to people. Refusing the spoils was a refusal to be bought.
    1 Timothy 6:10 — “For the love of money is a root of all sorts of injurious things, and by reaching out for this love some have been led astray from the faith”
  • He did not impose his conscience on others. His allies were free to take their portion (Gen. 14:24). A personal standard, held personally.
4.How can this account benefit me today?
Heart of itReal courage usually has a boundary around it. Abraham knew what he was fighting for, and he knew when to stop.
  • Be quick when someone needs help. Whether Lot deserved rescuing never enters the account. Family in trouble was enough.
    Galatians 6:2 — “Go on carrying the burdens of one another, and in this way you will fulfill the law of the Christ.”
  • Help when it is in your power. Abraham had the men, the knowledge and the opportunity — which is precisely what creates the obligation.
    Proverbs 3:27 — “Do not withhold good from those to whom you should give it If it is within your power to help.”
  • Decide your limits before you are offered anything. Most compromises happen because the decision was left until the moment of pressure.
    Proverbs 22:1 — “A good name is to be chosen rather than great wealth; To be respected is better than silver and gold.”
  • Guard who gets the credit. He refused wealth rather than let a man of Sodom claim to have enriched him.
    1 Timothy 6:9 — “But those who are determined to be rich fall into temptation and a snare and many senseless and harmful desires that plunge men into destruction and ruin.”
  • Courage and planning are not opposites. Dividing his forces and attacking at night was smart, not timid. Wisdom does not dilute bravery.
  • Know when the objective is met. He rescued and went home. He did not turn a justified action into an appetite.

Ask yourself: Have I actually decided, in advance, what I will not accept — or am I planning to work it out when the offer arrives?
