Abraham — He Faced His Hardest Challenge

“Do not harm the boy, and do not do anything at all to him, for now I do know that you are God-fearing because you have not withheld your son, your only one, from me.”
Genesis 22:12

Focus. Abraham received an instruction that contradicted everything he had been promised, and then walked for three days carrying it. The test was not a single moment of nerve — it was 72 hours of continuing to walk.

1.Create context — when, who, and what was going on

When

This is usually placed around 1893 BCE, when Isaac was roughly 25 — old enough to carry the wood up a mountain, and strong enough to have resisted a father of about 125 had he chosen to. Isaac's cooperation is part of the account, not an afterthought. (His exact age is not stated; 25 is an inference.)

The journey was to “the land of Moriah” (Gen. 22:2) — about three days' travel (Gen. 22:4). A sudden act done in a rush of feeling is one thing; three days of walking, camping and rising again is another entirely.

Timeline — promise, test, oath

1918 BCE Isaac born when Abraham is 100 (Gen. 21:5).

What was going on

The instruction was designed to be as costly as words allow: “your son, your only son whom you so love, Isaac” (Gen. 22:2). Each phrase tightens the knot.

And it appeared to cancel the promise. Everything depended on this boy — “What will be called your offspring will be through Isaac” (Heb. 11:18). Obeying looked like destroying the very thing God had promised to build.

2.What was Jehovah’s view of the situation?
Heart of itJehovah never intended Isaac to die. He was looking for something he could only see by asking — and he asked nothing he was not later willing to do himself.

It was a test, and Genesis says so from the start (Gen. 22:1). The reader is told immediately what Abraham was not told.

He stopped it at the last possible second. Not when Abraham set out, but with the knife already raised (Gen. 22:10, 11). The intention had to be complete to be real.

Genesis 22:12

“Then he said: ‘Do not harm the boy, and do not do anything at all to him, for now I do know that you are God-fearing because you have not withheld your son, your only one, from me.’”

He responded with an oath. Jehovah rarely swears. Here he does: “By myself I swear” (Gen. 22:16).

He asked nothing he would not face himself. This is the detail that reframes the whole account. Centuries later Jehovah did give up his own Son, and no angel called out to stop it.

3.What was Abraham doing — and how did he cope?
Heart of itHe obeyed before he understood, and he used the three days to reason rather than to panic.

He answered immediately. Twice he says “Here I am!” (Gen. 22:1, 11) — once to receive the instruction and once to be released from it.

He reasoned his way to a conclusion. Hebrews tells us exactly what he worked out: God “was able to raise him up even from the dead” (Heb. 11:19). This was not blind obedience.

He said so out loud. To the servants: “the boy and I will go over there and worship and return to you” (Gen. 22:5).

He answered his son honestly. “God himself will provide the sheep” (Gen. 22:8). It was true in a way he could not yet see.

How did he cope?

  • He reasoned from what he already knew of God. He had watched God produce a child from two bodies past the age of childbearing.
    Romans 4:21 — “and being fully convinced that what He had promised He was also able to do.”
  • He trusted where he could not calculate. The instruction made no sense on his own understanding — which is exactly the situation Proverbs addresses.
    Proverbs 3:5 — “Trust in Jehovah with all your heart, And do not rely on your own understanding.”
  • He accepted that God's reasoning runs higher than his. The gap between what he was told and what he could see was the whole test.
    Isaiah 55:9 — “For as the heavens are higher than the earth, So my ways are higher than your ways And my thoughts than your thoughts.”
  • He kept moving. Building the altar, arranging the wood — ordinary actions done in order (Gen. 22:9). Sometimes faithfulness looks like completing the next task.
4.How can this account benefit me today?
Heart of itFaith is not the absence of reasoning. Abraham thought his way through this, and the thinking is recorded so we can copy it.
  • Obedience sometimes comes before understanding. Abraham was given the instruction; the explanation came afterwards.
    Proverbs 3:5, 6 — “Trust in Jehovah with all your heart, And do not rely on your own understanding. In all your ways take notice of him, And he will make your paths straight.”
  • God asks nothing of you that he was unwilling to bear himself. Abraham got his son back. Jehovah did not.
    Romans 8:32 — “Since he did not even spare his own Son but handed him over for us all, will he not also, along with him, kindly give us all other things?”
  • Hard tests are not evidence of God's displeasure. This was asked of a man with roughly a century of proven faith behind him.
    James 1:2, 3 — “Consider it all joy, my brothers, when you meet with various trials, knowing as you do that this tested quality of your faith produces endurance.”
  • Being God's friend is a real category. Friendship with God is built by what you do, not only by what you believe.
    James 2:23 — “Abraham put faith in Jehovah, and it was counted to him as righteousness, and he came to be called Jehovah's friend.”
  • Reason from what God has already done. When something makes no sense, the useful question is not “why?” but “what do I already know about him?”
  • Tell the truth even when it is incomplete. “God himself will provide” was honest, and it held.

Ask yourself: When something Jehovah asks does not make sense to me, do I reason from what I already know of him — or do I simply stall?
